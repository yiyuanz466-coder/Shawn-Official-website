## Context

**OpenHCI** is a human-computer interaction design workshop in Taiwan where teams go from research and ideation to an interactive prototype in a short time. Our team chose bedtime stories: a moment almost every family shares, yet one that is rarely redesigned.

## Problem

For children aged **3–7**, building critical thinking and self-expression is essential. Yet most story players and parenting media **only play content one way**, with no interactive context, so children absorb passively and struggle to internalize what they learn or express their feelings.

Parents, meanwhile, face information overload and limited time. Guiding a child through everyday topics, emotions and values often means designing activities or finding content themselves, **a heavy cognitive load**.

<Callout title="Design question">
How might we turn bedtime stories from one-way playback into an interactive ritual that parents and children create together, while lightening the load on parents?
</Callout>

## Research & insights

Child development research gave us two design anchors:

- Children aged **4–7** are in the pre-schematic stage, and their drawings come from conscious observation of real life (Lowenfeld, 1947). In other words, **a child's drawing is a window into their thinking**.
- Children aged **3–6** benefit from picture books with a clear narrative arc, which build attention and comprehension (Mol, 2011).

<Callout title="Key insight">
If the story starts from the child's own drawing and grows from the family's conversation, the child becomes a co-author, and parents get to hear what their child really thinks.
</Callout>

## Ideation

We converged on **a daily bedtime-story ritual built around AI co-creation**, with three design goals:

## Design & iteration

The product combines a **UFO-shaped storytelling device** with an **app**. The UFO echoes the "story planet" world, and a light-up start cue gives storytelling a sense of ritual.

<Figure src="images/device.jpg" alt="Two UFO-shaped storytelling devices with clear domes and glowing bases" caption="The UFO storyteller: light marks the start, and parents and kids can adjust the projection angle and focus themselves." />

The experience has three steps:

1. **Scan the child's drawing to generate a unique story**: AI uses the drawing and the child's words to create a planet and an opening story.
2. **Co-create the story with an AI agent**: AI answers questions from parents and children and steers the story in new directions based on the conversation.
3. **Planet journal & recap**: key discussions, insights into the child's thinking and suggestions are summarized each day for the family to revisit together in the app.

<Figure src="images/flow.jpg" alt="Three steps: the UFO device projects light onto a child's drawing to scan it, the device shows AI story-generation progress, and a phone app shows the planet journal" caption="Scan the drawing → co-create with AI → revisit the planet journal." wide />

We also designed a cast of characters so children always have familiar companions in the story world.

<Figure src="images/characters.jpg" alt="Four character illustrations: a white mascot with a planetary ring reading a book, a blue monster, a pink ghost and a yellow star" caption="DearPlanet's mascot and story characters." wide />

## Solution

The system runs two pipelines:

- **Drawing scan & planet generation**: an ESP32 captures the drawing in real time, **KMeans clustering** extracts its main colors, generative AI renders the planet, and **Three.js** displays it.
- **Interactive story generation**: AI writes the opening story, then **gesture** and **speech recognition** let the family pause, continue or regenerate the story mid-conversation.

## Results

- Won the **Best Interaction Award** at **OpenHCI 2025**.
- Delivered a working prototype that connects drawing scan, planet generation, the AI picture book and the planet journal into one parent-child story experience.

## Reflection

This project taught me that a good AI experience doesn't replace parents as storytellers; it **sits between parent and child** as a prompt for conversation. The child's drawings and answers are the real heroes of the story.

If I took it further, I would:

- Run a long-term study with real families to see whether the bedtime ritual sticks and whether parent-child conversations improve.
- Design stronger safety checks for AI-generated content so stories are always appropriate for young children.
- Make the planet journal's analysis more concrete, so it genuinely helps parents understand their child's feelings and ideas.
