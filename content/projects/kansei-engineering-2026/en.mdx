## Context

Most interactive products are optimized for speed and zero friction. Yet in everyday rituals such as drinking a cup of tea, meaning often lives in the pause itself: the weight of the glass, the care in setting it down.

Slow Design argues for deliberately adding **behavioral friction**, resistance that demands conscious engagement, so people stop and return to the present. Strauss and Fuad-Luke describe six principles: Reveal, Expand, Reflect, Engage, Participate and Evolve.

However, most prior work **assumes slowness is inherently positive** and rarely asks how the same slow interaction is perceived across different social contexts.

## Problem

<Callout title="Research question">
How can behavioral friction be deliberately embedded into a familiar everyday object to support ritual and reflection, and how is its value shaped by social context?
</Callout>

- **RQ1**: How can Kansei Engineering and Slow Design principles translate abstract "slowness" into a concrete interactive artifact?
- **RQ2**: Does the friction that fosters mindful reflection in solitary use disrupt the flow of interaction in group settings?

## Research & insights

We followed a **Research through Design** approach in three phases: quantitative Kansei Engineering → prototype development → situated evaluation.

### Phase I: Finding the empty space with Kansei Engineering

We observed how people handle tableware in bars, coffee shops and homes, then curated 15 representative coaster samples.

<Figure src="images/coaster-samples.jpg" alt="Fifteen diverse coaster samples, including vinyl-record, wooden, glass, glowing and cartoon designs" caption="The 15 coaster samples used in the semantic differential survey." wide />

We built a semantic differential (SD) scale of **10 bipolar adjective pairs** mapped to five Slow Design principles, and replaced academic terms with plain wording after a 5-person pilot. Thirty-two participants then rated every sample on a seven-point scale.

**Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)** revealed three Kansei factors explaining **87.94%** of the variance:

<Callout title="Key insight">
Coasters that combine **emotional richness, high interactivity and humor** barely exist. That gap is where slow design can step in.
</Callout>

## Ideation

Guided by this gap, we split the Slow Design principles into two layers of friction:

- **Physical: visceral friction**. Handcrafted, irregular glass with uneven weight and texture can't be set down carelessly. An embedded LED provides ambient feedback.
- **Digital: reflective pacing**. The app pushes back against "instant access" and asks users to pause and notice how they feel.

## Design & iteration

The result is the **Slow Coaster**: a handcrafted glass coaster with embedded NFC and LED, paired with a web app.

<Figure src="images/coaster-app.jpg" alt="A phone held over a glowing blue glass coaster, showing the app's launch screen" caption="Tapping the phone on the coaster is the only way in, a deliberate hands-on step." />

Four mechanisms each carry one slow-design strategy:

1. **High-friction entry**: users must physically scan the coaster to launch the app, breaking the stimulus-response loop of notifications.
2. **Temporal buffering**: the app loads at a deliberate pace, turning waiting into a moment of transition.
3. **Emotional mirroring**: users log their mood, and the coaster's light color and an AI-curated playlist respond.
4. **AI & social connection**: users revisit their mood and music history and see traces left by previous users of the same coaster.

<Figure src="images/interaction-flow.jpg" alt="Interaction flow: A scan the coaster to launch the app, B slow loading screen, C log your mood, D the coaster lights up and plays a playlist, E browse past entries and shared history" caption="Core interaction flow: scan, slow launch, mood logging, then light and music feedback." wide />

## Solution

The Slow Coaster turns slowness into three layers of experience:

- **Slowness you can touch**: the weight and texture of irregular glass make setting a cup down a conscious act.
- **Slowness you can see**: ambient light quietly mirrors your mood; you can notice it or ignore it.
- **Slowness you choose**: scanning and mood logging are reflective moments the user opts into.

## Results

We tested the prototype in two contrasting settings: a lively **commercial bar** (N=5, including a bartender, a waitress and the owner) and a quiet **living room** (N=3). Light feedback was simulated with a Wizard-of-Oz protocol. Each session lasted about 20 minutes and was followed by a semi-structured interview and thematic analysis.

Everyone loved the glass and the light, but the explicit app interaction split opinions sharply:

- **Healing in solitude**: participants described the experience as "healing", helping them clear their minds and relax. The bar owner said it turned waiting for a drink into a "ritualistic experience" for solo guests.
- **Disruption in company**: 6 of 8 participants felt slow interaction was unwelcome in social settings, because looking at a phone cut off the conversation.
- **Ambient light as an icebreaker**: light colors and mood history became "topic-openers" with friends and even strangers.

> "When friends are there, I don't want to spend time selecting my mood — I want to interact with them." — P6

## Reflection

**Slowness is not inherently good; its value depends on social context.** Friction should be a calibrated design choice, not a universal rule.

The study also has limitations that point to next steps:

- AI recommendations and light feedback were simulated with predefined playlists and Wizard-of-Oz. A real AI backend is needed to test long-term personalization.
- The social history used pre-built data; a live deployment needs clear consent and privacy design.
- Eight participants in single 20-minute sessions only capture ritual initiation. Longer, larger studies are needed to see whether annoying friction becomes a ritual through habit.
- The "humor" identified in the Kansei analysis is hard to deliver through digital feedback alone. Combining the responsiveness of AI software with the symbolic meaning of physical objects is a promising direction.
